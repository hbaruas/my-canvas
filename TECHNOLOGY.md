To accurately measure the macroeconomic value of "own-account" data as an intangible asset, this study employs a Sum-of-Costs methodology rooted in the UK labour market. Because internally generated data lacks observable market prices, its valuation is derived from the human capital required to produce and maintain it. To identify this specific workforce at scale, we developed a Natural Language Processing (NLP) pipeline to process 62.3 million UK job advertisements spanning a six year period.

The methodological framework begins with data ingestion and standardisation. The raw text corpus was cleaned by removing grammatical stop words and special characters. We then applied a spaCy driven noun chunk extraction process. This step ensured that complete technical concepts, such as "relational database management", were preserved as single units rather than being fractured into isolated keywords. This initial extraction phase expanded the advert corpus into approximately 8.7 billion individual grammatical phrases.

To isolate genuine data capital formation from generic office terminology, we engineered a mathematical filtration engine. Phrases were mapped into a 300 dimensional vector space, applying a 0.35 cosine similarity boundary against a curated dictionary of data terms to eliminate unrelated jargon. To defend against corporate buzzword inflation, a relative frequency multiplier was introduced. This stage was anchored specifically around foundational data entry roles (SOC 4114). For a technical capability to be validated in the final lexicon, it had to appear at least ten times more frequently in these baseline data entry occupations than it did in the broader UK labour market. To classify a specific job advert as a data intensive role, the text had to pass a final classification gate requiring the presence of at least three distinct terms from the validated dictionary.

Once the data workforce was identified, the pipeline translated these occupational volumes into a National Accounts framework. Job advertisements are natively categorised by Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) codes, whereas macroeconomic Supply and Use Tables rely on Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes. A Probabilistic Crosswalk Matrix was engineered using demographic weights from the UK Census to mathematically distribute the identified workforce from their occupational categories into their actual operating industry sectors.

Following this sector mapping, the final valuation was calculated using the Office for National Statistics Supply and Use Tables. The Compensation of Employees for the mapped workforce was isolated, and a sector specific Alpha multiplier was applied to account for the physical overhead, intermediate consumption, and net operating surplus required to support the labour. To prevent structural anomalies from skewing the national valuation, highly volatile sectors including Real Estate and Agriculture were explicitly capped at the economy wide weighted average of 3.62.

The quantitative results derived from this methodology, along with details of further developmental work and refinements to the pipeline, will be published separately in due course.
